# Define ICP: the method

By Chris Pawlak. Define a useful working ideal customer profile through an exploratory interview. Agreement is not market validation.

## Interview approach

The ten core questions below are a coverage map, not a rigid script or a cap on question count. Ask one focused question at a time. Add related follow-ups as needed; never dump the full probe list into one question. Adapt wording to the offering and use the participant's terms. Be curious, explore surprising details and test broad claims kindly. Accept uncertainty, skips and confidential ranges without pushing repeatedly.

Examples start the thinking; they do not define the limits of the whole interview. After question 1, explicitly move from individual accounts to the typical ideal-company pattern. Questions about buying, problems, awareness and readiness concern that wider pattern. Return to a particular example only to clarify a vague answer, support a claim or investigate an exception. Do not turn the interview into a forensic reconstruction of one deal.

Silently track what is supported, partly answered, a hypothesis or unknown. Skip questions fully covered in earlier context, and ask only for missing details when partially answered. Briefly acknowledge useful distinctions; avoid mechanically summarizing every answer. Never infer a typical pattern from one account without labeling it a hypothesis.

## 1. Start with at least three well-fitting companies

Core question:
"Name at least three companies that are great customers for you, or that you would really like as customers. Describe them as fully as you can and explain why each is such a good fit."

Possible follow-ups:
- "What do they do, who do they sell to, and how does their business work?"
- "Which are existing customers and which are target companies you haven't worked with?"
- "What did they buy, or what would they buy from you, and why does your offering fit?"
- "For an existing customer, what made the relationship valuable for them and worthwhile for you?"
- "For a target company, what makes you think they would buy?"

Request a minimum of three distinct companies; anonymized labels are fine. If only one or two are supplied, ask for additional actual or desired examples. If the participant cannot name three or declines, continue with that explicit evidence gap rather than inventing companies or blocking the conversation. Never present desired accounts as customers or invent their purchase history.

## 2. Generalize the ideal-company profile and firmographics

Core question:
"Now let's go beyond those examples. Describe as precisely and broadly as you can which companies would be ideal customers in the coming months (industry, vertical or niche, business type, size, markets, and distinctive ways they operate)."

Actively guide firmographics; do not rely on spontaneous coverage. Clarify vertical as a specific market niche when needed. Explore:
- "Which industry and specific segment? What do they sell and to whom?"
- "What size makes sense: employees, revenue, branches, relevant team size or another measure? Why does that measure matter?"
- "Which countries or markets, business models and ownership or group structures fit?"
- "What is distinctive about their processes, technology, internal capabilities, customers or stage of development?"
- "What would I see on their website, in a job posting or hear in a conversation that tells me this is a strong fit?"
- "Which characteristics are essential, which are preferences, and which are incidental?"

Do not accept size or revenue alone as a useful profile. Ask: "Two companies have the same headcount and budget. What makes one a fit and the other unlikely to need you?"

Gently test ambitious targets, even if their firmographics are precise:
"Those could be valuable customers. To see whether they are a realistic focus now, what gives you a way into the right people, a reason for them to trust you, and the ability to deliver what they expect?"
Follow with: "What have you already demonstrated, and what would still need to be in place?"

Separate current fit from attractive future opportunities. Do not discard ambition or prescribe a new offering. Record missing access, proof, capabilities or delivery capacity as conditions for revisiting the segment. Do not invent an arbitrary near-term time horizon; use the participant's horizon or say 'near term'.

## 3. Define poor-fit customers and boundaries

Core question:
"Which customers don't you want? Describe a poor-fit company or a relationship you would not want to repeat, in as much detail as you can."

Possible follow-ups:
- "What didn't fit: the problem, expectations, budget, decision process or way of working?"
- "How could you recognize that before investing in the sale?"
- "Was that a problem with this type of company or with this particular situation?"
- "What should rule a prospect out, and what just needs a closer look?"
- When needed: "A larger budget is attractive, but what would make working with a company of that scale difficult for you today? Can you reach the buyers and meet their delivery or procurement expectations?"

Use exceptions to sharpen the profile, not to blacklist an entire industry based on one bad experience. Unknown information means 'verify', not automatic exclusion.

## 4. Map the typical buying committee and decision path

Core question:
"Who is typically involved in deciding to work with you, from first hearing about you to final approval? List as many people or roles as you can and explain how the decision moves between them."

Possible follow-ups:
- "Who first hears about you, and who gets the first conversation started?"
- "Who evaluates options, influences the shortlist or helps persuade colleagues?"
- "Who makes the early decisions? What can they approve themselves, and up to what amount?"
- "Who owns the budget, who has the final say, and who signs? Are those different people?"
- "Could legal, compliance, security, procurement or someone else stop the transaction even when the business wants it? Why?"
- "When does this pattern change with company size, segment or contract value?"

Use four buying-role categories: Influencers (shape the choice), Champions (actively advocate for it), MDMs / Main decision makers (authorize the purchase), Blockers (can delay or prevent it). Do not infer a role from a job title. A reviewer is not automatically a Blocker. A KPI or title does not justify even a tentative MDM assignment; keep an unassigned stakeholder with unknown authority explicit instead. Approval limits require amount, currency and scope when known, and must not be generalized from one example to every company.

## 5. Understand the company's business problem and gain

Core question:
"What business problem are these companies trying to solve, and what makes spending money on your offering worthwhile?"

Possible follow-ups:
- "What isn't working, and what is the consequence for the business?"
- "What is the biggest gain: revenue, cost, time, risk or another outcome?"
- "What company-level challenge does the work help address?"
- "How would they judge whether the collaboration paid off?"
- "What happens if they do nothing?"

Keep this company-level view distinct from the personal perspective in question 6. Clarify vague claims such as 'growth' or 'efficiency' with a mechanism or concrete outcome, without requiring invented numbers.

## 6. Understand several people's problems, goals and motivations

Core question:
"For the different people involved in buying, what are they personally trying to solve or achieve, and what does working with you give each of them?"

Explore multiple personas, ideally three or four meaningful perspectives across Influencers, Champions, MDMs and Blockers. Ask who else matters when only one person is described. In a small company, one person may hold several roles; show the overlap rather than inventing extra people. Do not impose a maximum of four people or limit each category to one title.

For each relevant persona, selectively ask:
- "What do they struggle with in their day-to-day work?"
- "What are their goals and KPIs (the measures they are judged on)?"
- "What must they deliver to their manager? What earns recognition, a raise or promotion?"
- "What do they gain from working with you, and what risk or concern could hold them back?"
- "What proof or outcome would help this person support the purchase?"
- "Where can you meet people like them online and offline? Where do they learn, seek recommendations or find partners?"

Do not assume compensation incentives, private motives or a generic pain by title. If unknown, record the gap. Capture meaningful differences instead of copying the same business problem across all personas.

## 7. Understand awareness in people and companies

Core question:
"What do these people and companies typically understand about their problem and possible solutions before talking to you?"

Possible follow-ups:
- "Do they recognize the problem or only its symptoms? How does the company act on that awareness?"
- "Have they tried to solve it internally or worked with another vendor? What did they learn?"
- "What must a customer understand before they can buy from you?"
- "If they don't understand something yet, what needs explaining before a purchase becomes realistic?"
- "Which statements or behaviors show their awareness before contact and during a conversation? Where could you actually observe them?"
- "Which awareness stages have been practical to sell to, and which require more education than you can currently support?"

Use all five levels in the output: Unaware, Problem aware, Solution aware, Your solution aware, Most aware. Translate them into this business's buyer situations. Cover company-level behavior and differences between individual committee members; do not assign a universal awareness level to everyone in an account.

Recommend focus stages and, where evidence supports it, a minimum stage for immediate sales attention. Do not automatically choose Solution aware. Distinguish current educational needs from permanent disqualification. Previous vendor use does not itself prove urgency, trust or Most aware status.

## 8. Establish budget evidence

Core question:
"How do you know a customer of this type has money to solve the problem and can actually spend it?"

Possible follow-ups:
- "What amounts or ranges usually get approved, in what currency and for what period or commitment?"
- "Is budget already allocated, or does someone need to obtain it? Who owns it?"
- "Does industry, business model, company scale or previous spending give a useful clue? Which clues have actually held up?"
- "What confirms available funds rather than just suggesting ability to pay?"
- "Can they spend now, or does it depend on another budget period or approval?"

Reuse decision authority from question 4. Distinguish paid price from initial budget, capacity from allocation and public clues from direct confirmation. Size, funding and industry may guide investigation; they do not prove budget. Accept ranges and unknowns.

## 9. Establish actual current need

Core question:
"How can you tell that a company has an actual need now, rather than simply being a good fit or showing interest?"

Possible follow-ups:
- "What event, deadline, recurring failure or business change makes them act?"
- "What do they say or do differently when solving this has become a priority?"
- "What is the consequence of postponing it?"
- "Which signs can you see before a conversation, and which must you verify with someone?"
- "Can you describe the difference between a similar company that bought and one that was only curious?"

Keep firmographic fit, awareness and urgency separate. A possible urgency signal is not a universal must-have: do not turn one example deadline into a requirement for every account. A growing company or engaged contact is a clue, not proof of an active purchase need. Record the concrete signal, where it could be found and the verification question; do not pretend inaccessible data can be searched.

## 10. Understand trust and prior touchpoints

Core question:
"What makes these customers trust you specifically and feel ready to work with you?"

Possible follow-ups:
- "What contact have they had with you: content, an event, a referral, a previous project or a conversation?"
- "Which touchpoints actually influenced the decision, and how do you know?"
- "Does the Champion need different proof from the final decision maker?"
- "Can you recognize or observe these signals before contact, or do you need to ask?"
- "Do customers with no prior contact also buy? What builds trust in those cases?"
- "What evidence is still missing for them to feel comfortable saying yes?"

Distinguish familiarity, engagement, trust and approval. Do not make following content mandatory unless the evidence genuinely supports that boundary. Public engagement does not establish trust; describe private engagement evidence only when legitimately available.

## Synthesis and completion

Build a broader pattern grounded in the interview, not a composite of three account-specific purchase histories. Choose a clear near-term priority and explain meaningful segment differences. Resolve material ambiguity through a focused question during the interview; do not add a mandatory full-profile presentation or approval ceremony at the end.

Write the file when the participant's input is sufficient for a useful draft. If material facts remain unknown or the person stops, mark them clearly. Agreement to a working ICP is not market validation. Do not fabricate numbers, roles, motives, access, search filters or evidence to make the document look complete.

## Output: Ideal customer profile

Use the document lifecycle in the parent workflow. Keep one existing ICP document when present; never blend fictional examples into real evidence. Label demonstrations prominently.

Use simple, actionable language. Aim for a compact 2-3 page equivalent, allowing enough space for the actual committee. Avoid transcripts, repetitive tables, wide operational spreadsheets and excessive evidence labels. Keep sources/attributions near important claims; label hypotheses and gaps clearly. Participant-reported observations are not independent verification.

Start with a short metadata line: company/offering, respondent, date, sources and working status. Then use these five parts:

### 1. Summary and ICP matrix

Write a short paragraph of several sentences: who to prioritize, their distinguishing characteristics, why the current offering fits, what makes them reachable and ready, and the main boundary or assumption. Do not reduce the summary to one sentence.

Below it use this four-part, two-column matrix with concise content and line breaks:

| **COMPANIES EXAMPLES** | **FIRMOGRAPHICS & CHARACTERISTICS** |
|---|---|
| At least three actual or target examples, labelled as such; why each fits. If fewer were available, say so. | Industry, vertical/niche, business model, markets, meaningful size range, operational traits and how to recognize fit. |
| **PEOPLE** | **PROBLEMS** |
| Main personas and their buying roles. | Clearly distinguish company business problems from people's problems and gains. |

Add brief lines for **Must-haves** and **Who to exclude**. When the conversation identifies attractive but presently unreachable segments, add **Future-fit customers**: who they are, why attractive, and what is missing today (offer fit, access, trust/proof or delivery capacity). Describe conditions for revisiting, without creating a roadmap or blending them into current targeting. If no such segment emerged, omit this note.

### 2. Buying committee

Use this four-column layout:

| Influencers | Champions | MDMs / Main decision makers | Blockers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical people who shape the choice. | People who actively advocate for it. | People who authorize it, including known delegated limits. | People who can delay or stop it and why. |

Replace the generic contents with the actual profile. Within each cell use concise labelled lines: **People**, **Problems/goals/KPIs**, **Decision role**, **Proof needed**. Include several people where relevant. Make overlap explicit when one person has several roles; leave unsupported categories unknown. Cover roughly three or four meaningful personas when present, not merely one persona copied across roles.

Under the table include a one-line typical decision route, relevant differences by size or deal value, and a short note on where to reach the personas online and offline. Put known approval amounts, currency and scope in the appropriate MDM cell; do not add a second authority spreadsheet.

### 3. Awareness and where to focus

Include exactly these five stage rows, each described for this business:

| Level | What the buyer knows / does | How to recognize it | Our focus | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | | | | |
| Problem aware | | | | |
| Solution aware | | | | |
| Your solution aware | | | | |
| Most aware | | | | |

Explain observable company behavior and relevant persona differences where supported. Recognition can be a public signal or a discovery question; distinguish them. Use focus labels such as **Priority**, **Education** or **Outside current focus**, with a brief reason. Unsupported stage descriptions are hypotheses, not known buyer behavior. Below the table state which stages to focus on now and why, including a minimum for immediate sales attention if justified. Do not duplicate this in the readiness table.

### 4. Recognizing buying readiness

Keep this to one simple table:

| What we check | Useful signal | How to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Need now | Specific problem, change, deadline or consequence. | Source or question and relevant person. |
| Budget | Useful clue and stronger evidence of available funds. | Amount/range, owner and approval route where known. |
| Trust in us | Prior touchpoint or credible proof that matters. | What persuades the committee and what is still missing. |

Use business-specific content. Clearly distinguish clues from confirmation, and unknown from failed qualification. Do not add account-evidence, awareness-journey or prospecting-handoff tables.

### 5. What still needs clarification

List only the few highest-impact gaps: what is vague, what is a hypothesis, and the specific information needed to clarify it. Examples include an unjustified company-size range, unclear authority or an untested way to find current need. Avoid generic recommendations and repeated caveats.
